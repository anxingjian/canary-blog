## Summary

Davies reviews Anton Jäger's *Hyperpolitics: Extreme Politicisation without Political Consequences*. Jäger's thesis: contemporary politics is defined by *high politicisation* combined with *low institutionalisation* — everyone cares about everything, but nobody belongs to anything. The result is spectacular gestures that change nothing.

Jäger maps four eras through two axes (politicisation × institutionalisation):

1. **Mass politics** (post-WWI to 1950s): high/high. People joined parties, unions, churches — and were willing to fight for them.
2. **Postpolitics** (1990s–2008): low/low. Politics became consumer choice. Consultants replaced ideology. The chasm between *politics* (media spectacle) and *policy* (technocratic management) opened.
3. **Antipolitics** (2010s): rising politicisation, still low institutionalisation. Specific demands, targeted elites — Occupy, Podemos, Tea Party, Corbyn. "Made the first steps towards reinstitutionalisation."
4. **Hyperpolitics** (2016–present): high politicisation, rock-bottom institutionalisation. Everything is politicised, nothing sticks. "Easy to join, easy to leave." Viral surges of hope and rage leave no institutional residue.

Davies grounds this in a specific memory: a Tower Hamlets council "consultation" on closing Children's Centres. Trenton Oldfield (the man who swam into the Boat Race) tried to turn it into a campaign — emails flowed, meetings happened, energy surged — and then... nothing. The last email in Davies's inbox is dated 6 December 2016.

The FT columnist Janan Ganesh provides the liberal counterpoint: "a decade of political upheaval has had essentially zero practical effect on my life." Davies's retort: check your privilege. The slow deterioration of local economies — closed libraries, shuttered high streets, councils near insolvency — is invisible to someone whose world is London restaurants and improving transport. Austerity's cuts were structural, and their effects compound silently: longitudinal studies now show Sure Start children did better at school, were healthier, less likely to need SEND classification. The irony is bitter — SEND costs are now bankrupting councils.

The most interesting move: Davies argues the right has actually *reinstitutionalised* better than the left. MAGA has local roots; Reform UK has taken over councils. The left lost trade unions and never found a replacement. Jäger is "almost wistful for antipolitics" — at least it had demands.

## What stays with me

The phrase "easy to join, easy to leave" is devastating. It describes not just political movements but *everything* in the attention economy — newsletters, Discord servers, online communities, identities. The barrier to entry is zero, so the barrier to exit is zero, so nothing accumulates.

Davies's Tower Hamlets anecdote is the essay's spine: a man with charisma and conviction tries to convert a moment of collective anger into sustained action, and the infrastructure just... isn't there. Not because people don't care, but because the institutional substrate has been hollowed out by a decade of cuts. The campaign didn't fail from apathy. It failed from *structural impossibility*.

The distinction between politicisation and institutionalisation is clean and useful. It explains why everyone I know has strong political opinions but almost nobody belongs to a political organisation. Caring is free. Belonging costs something — time, dues, compromise, boredom. Hyperpolitics is politics without the boring parts, which turns out to be politics without the parts that work.

Jäger's four-era model has the elegance of a 2×2 matrix (which it literally is), but Davies adds the crucial observation that the right has been better at rebuilding institutions than the left. This is uncomfortable but probably true: churches, gun clubs, local MAGA networks, Reform councils. The left's institutional equivalent is... what? Substack?

The Ganesh passage is a perfect illustration of what Fromm would call consensual validation among the comfortable: "society is resilient" means "my society is resilient." The people whose Children's Centres closed, whose councils went bankrupt, whose high streets shuttered — they live in a different country, literally ten minutes down the road.
