# Embodied Cognition and the Grip of Computational Metaphors

**Author:** K. Finley
**Source:** *Ergo* 12, 117–159 (2025)
**Read:** 2026-05-12
**Domain:** Philosophy of Cognitive Science

## Core Argument

Finley argues that computational metaphors—treating the mind as a program, cognition as information processing, neural activity as "computing"—don't merely *describe* cognition but actively *constrain* how embodied cognition research is conceived and practiced. Even researchers who explicitly reject computationalism often smuggle computational assumptions back in through their framing: speaking of "representations" being "processed," of sensorimotor "codes," of the body as an "interface."

The paper's central thesis: embodied cognition hasn't fully escaped the computational paradigm because the *language* of cognitive science was forged inside that paradigm. You can't think outside the box if the box is the language you think in.

## Key Moves

1. **Metaphor as infrastructure, not decoration.** Finley draws on Lakoff & Johnson but pushes further: computational metaphors aren't just convenient shorthand, they're load-bearing walls in the theoretical architecture. Remove them and many "embodied" claims collapse into vagueness.

2. **The grip problem.** Echoing Merleau-Ponty's "optimal grip" (which Hovhannisyan 2026 develops separately), Finley uses "grip" in a double sense: computational metaphors have a *grip on us* (we can't let them go) precisely because they promise the kind of precise, formal grip on phenomena that science demands. Embodied alternatives often feel like they're trading explanation for hand-waving.

3. **The formalization trap.** To be taken seriously as science, embodied cognition must formalize its claims—but formalization itself tends to reintroduce computational structure. Dynamical systems approaches partially escape this, but even they import assumptions about state spaces and trajectories that are "computation-adjacent."

4. **A proposed way out:** Finley suggests that embodied cognitive science should embrace *narrative explanation* and *case-based reasoning* as legitimate scientific modes, rather than always seeking the covering-law model that computationalism provides so naturally. This connects to phenomenological psychology's methods.
