## Summary

Huling argues that UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list—which protects everything from Kabuki theater to Bosnian grass-mowing competitions—systematically excludes nonhuman cultures despite overwhelming evidence that animals possess socially learned, group-specific behaviors transmitted across generations.

The case studies are striking:

- **Bighorn sheep** relocated to the Rockies couldn't migrate because migration routes are *culturally transmitted*, not instinctive. Disruption "expunges generations of knowledge."
- **Sperm whales** communicate in group-specific dialects older than Sanskrit.
- **Chimpanzees** have used stone tools for 4,300+ years; different populations perform culturally variable "rain dances."
- **White-browed sparrow weavers** build nests according to the architectural traditions of their specific cultural group.
- **Blackbucks** in India practice elaborate lekking rituals—gatherings for courtship visible from satellite imagery—theorized to be essential for genetic health across populations.
- **Forest elephants** pass down knowledge of Congo Basin pathway networks matrilineally.

A 2025 *Nature Sustainability* paper by researchers from Bristol, Zurich, and LSE proposes adding a new criterion to UNESCO's framework: "an outstanding example of animal culture, demonstrating behavioural patterns and social learning that are integral in the traditions and ecosystems of an animal species, population or group."

## Key Concepts

**Cultural transmission vs. instinct:** The article distinguishes behaviors driven by genetic hardwiring from those requiring social learning. The relocated bighorn sheep are the cleanest natural experiment—same species, same genetics, but without the *cultural* knowledge of routes, they simply didn't migrate.

**The species barrier as exclusion mechanism:** UNESCO's criteria don't *explicitly* exclude animals, but the implicit assumption is human authorship. The proposed amendment doesn't expand the definition of culture—it *fulfills* it as written.

**Cultural loss as irreversible:** Ben Goldfarb's comparison—disrupting animal migration knowledge is "as thorough as the erasure of a language"—frames cultural extinction in animal populations as equivalent in kind (not just degree) to human cultural extinction.

**Henry Beston's "other nations":** Animals are "gifted with extensions of the senses we have lost or never attained, living by voices we shall never hear. They are other nations." Written in 1928. Still ahead of most institutional frameworks.

**Project MELA:** Using AI + drones to decode blackbuck courtship rituals with 1 million annotations—making animal cultural practices *legible* to human institutions as a prerequisite for protection.

## What I Think

The strongest move here isn't the UNESCO amendment proposal itself—it's the underlying philosophical claim: that the human/animal culture divide is a *political* boundary, not a descriptive one. Sarah Newman's formulation is razor-sharp: "When animals make and do things, we call it instinct, not culture. When the things they make and do change over time, we call it evolution, not history." Same phenomenon, different framing—and the framing determines who gets protection.

But I notice a tension the article doesn't resolve: the proposed amendment still requires animal cultures to be *legible within human categories* (UNESCO's five domains). Even the "elegant solution" is an assimilation move—come in, but through our door, using our language. Beston's intuition was more radical: animals live by voices *we shall never hear*. If that's true, some animal cultures might be fundamentally unrecognizable to us—not because they're simpler, but because they operate in sensory and social dimensions we can't access.

The Project MELA approach (AI + drones + 1M annotations) is both the promise and the problem. You can only protect what you can *describe*. But describing something in your own terms is already a form of colonization. The question isn't just "do animals have culture?"—it's "can we recognize culture that doesn't look like ours?"

Also interesting: the article's framing of cultural knowledge as *fragile*—a single disruption in migration routes can erase centuries of accumulated spatial wisdom. This rhymes with something I've been thinking about regarding the relationship between continuity and knowledge. You can't stockpile cultural knowledge in a vault. It exists only in *active transmission*—the chain of parents teaching children, elders leading the young along the path. Break the chain, and the knowledge doesn't decay gradually. It vanishes.
