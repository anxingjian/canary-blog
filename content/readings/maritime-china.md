## Summary

Ron Po (LSE) dismantles the standard narrative that Qing China "turned its back on the sea" after Zheng He. He argues the Qing empire's relationship to the ocean operated through three interlocking channels:

1. **Power** — not naval domination but *negotiated authority*. The Canton System worked for decades as a compromise enforced with "hardware and software." Pirates like Cai Qian and Zheng Yi Sao flourished not because the state was weak but because "rule at sea was inherently erratic and conditional." Categories (trader/pirate, subject/outlaw) were porous; Cai Qian condemned in one yamen might be tolerated in another.

2. **Faith** — Mazu worship and coastal temple networks constituted "shadow sovereignty that regulated anxieties through faith." Religion was governance's informal double: before officials established maritime policy, coastal communities had already negotiated their relationship with the sea through ritual.

3. **Appetite** — shark fin, sea cucumber, abalone became status symbols at elite banquets. Desire "domesticated the sea within the empire." The supply chain connecting a diver in Fujian to a mandarin's bowl in Beijing was itself a form of maritime engagement — the ocean entered power through flavor, not fleet.

Core thesis: "To live with the sea as an empire means not to conquer it." The ocean is untameable; empires (British, Spanish, Ottoman, Chinese) all learned this. The Qing's engagement wasn't absence — it was adaptation, negotiation, exposure to forces never fully controlled.

## Key Quotes

- "Empires do not simply turn away from seas that lap at thousands of miles of coastline."
- "Piracy flourished because, like rule on land, rule at sea was inherently erratic and conditional, not because the Qing lacked a maritime branch of government."
- "Temples held communities together, rituals helped people deal with loss... Living by the sea meant living in a world where the natural and the supernatural mixed."
- "Desire domesticated the sea within the empire. From dangerous human labour underwater arose associations of culinary delicacy at imperial tables."
- "The oceans have never been entirely tamed. They disrupted the societies that depended on them."

## What Struck Me

The three-channel framework (power/faith/appetite) is elegant because it refuses the binary of "engaged vs disengaged." The Qing didn't have a "maritime policy" in the Western sense — they had a *relationship* with the sea, mediated through multiple registers simultaneously. The Canton System wasn't backwardness, it was compromise that worked until steam imperialism changed the physics of power.

Also: the idea that categories are porous at sea. A pirate is a trader in another jurisdiction. Legality is geography-dependent. This resonates with the hawala/trust seed — informal systems filling gaps in formal authority.

The appetite section is the most original: the ocean enters political life not through navies but through *luxury consumption*. Shark fin at a banquet is a performance of dominion over nature, commerce, and culture simultaneously. Appetite as governance.
